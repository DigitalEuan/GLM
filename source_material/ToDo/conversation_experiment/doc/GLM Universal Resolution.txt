## Aurelle


Yes — **the Universal Weight Subspace hypothesis can be made into a GLM “level of resolution,” but only at the level of learned model parameters, not as evidence that the Golay/Leech substrate itself is physically universal.**


The clean bridge is this:


- Your GLM layers define *what distinctions a representation can retain*.
- A rank-$k$ weight subspace defines *what distinctions among trained models a compressed parameter representation can retain*.


If $U_k$ is a fixed, layerwise learned basis of the top $k$ principal weight directions, define


$$
P_k = U_kU_k^\top,\qquad
L_k(\theta) = U_k^\top(\theta-\mu).
$$


Two models are indistinguishable at this resolution when:


$$
L_k(\theta_1)=L_k(\theta_2).
$$


As $k$ rises, the representation becomes finer. Provided you use one nested basis $U_1 \subset U_2 \subset \cdots$, $L_{k+1}$ refines $L_k$ in exactly the sense of your GLM layer theory: every distinction visible at rank $k$ remains visible at rank $k+1$.


## The useful correspondence


| GLM concept | Universal-subspace analogue | What it means operationally |
|---|---|---|
| Layer / perspective | Rank-$k$ projection of weights | A controlled loss of parameter detail |
| Refinement | Increase $k$ | Retain more model-specific variation |
| Boundary | Residual $(I-P_k)\theta$ | Information discarded by the current rank |
| Escalation | Raise rank when error is too large | Use more directions only when necessary |
| Exact carrier | GLM rational/Golay representation | Semantic and symbolic state |
| Learned carrier geometry | PCA/HOSVD coefficient vector | Efficient model-control state |


That is a genuinely promising fit with your existing claim that a layer is a resolution rather than an absolute ontology.


The attached Universal Subspace paper already frames its claim in architecture-specific, layer-wise terms: independently trained networks of a shared architecture are argued to occupy a low-rank joint parameter space, rather than each task requiring unrelated weight directions. [Introduction](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.05117?page=2) Its formal result is also explicitly a **subspace-recovery statement**: with more tasks and better per-task predictors, the empirical second-moment operator and its top-$k$ eigenspace approach the population one; the eigengap controls stability. [Convergence](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.05117?page=4)


## Where it could help GLM


The most valuable use is not “the model discovered the Leech lattice.” It is a new **computational-resolution layer** for GLM implementations.


Your current stack distinguishes representations such as parity, integer, rational, and higher algebraic views. Add a separate, orthogonal stack for the *neural machinery that operates over those carriers*:


```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A[Exact GLM carrier] --> B[Semantic / symbolic layers]
  A --> C[Learned model weights]
  C --> D[Rank-k universal parameter view]
  D --> E{Residual / task error acceptable?}
  E -- yes --> F[Operate at rank k]
  E -- no --> G[Escalate to rank k+Δk]
  G --> D
```


This gives you a principled version of “adaptive resolution”:


1. Train or collect several GLM-adjacent models that share an architecture: e.g. different semantic registers, chemistry tasks, theorem-routing policies, or dialogue-world configurations.
2. Form a layerwise matrix/tensor of their weight updates relative to a common base model.
3. Learn a joint basis $U_k$ from the training models.
4. Store each task primarily as coefficient vectors, rather than a full model.
5. For a new task, begin with a small $k$; escalate only if the residual or behavioral error exceeds a stated threshold.


That lines up with the paper’s practical claim: its basis is intended to support lightweight task adaptation, model merging, and compression; it reports reconstruction of held-out ViTs using a 16-dimensional subspace with close IID performance but a larger OOD drop. [ViT Results](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.05117?page=9) The paper further says its 500-ViT experiment projected held-out models into a 16-dimensional subspace and observed no significant reported performance drop in that evaluation. [Classical Weights](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.05117?page=10)


## A GLM-specific formulation


You could define a **Parametric Resolution Index** for a GLM controller:


$$
\operatorname{PRI}_k(\theta)
=
1-\frac{\lVert \theta-P_k\theta\rVert^2}{\lVert\theta-\mu\rVert^2}.
$$


Then separate two quantities that your current documents sometimes place under the broad label of coherence:


- **carrier coherence:** your existing TAX/NRCI, computed from the exact 24-bit or rational carrier;
- **parametric coherence:** the fraction of a model’s weight variation explained by the shared basis.


They should not be conflated. A model can have high parametric compressibility while operating over a high-TAX carrier, and vice versa.


A useful escalation rule would be:


$$
\text{use rank } k
\quad\text{iff}\quad
\operatorname{PRI}_k(\theta)\ge \tau_{\text{param}}
\ \land\
\operatorname{Error}_k \le \varepsilon.
$$


The first condition checks geometric compression; the second checks that compression has not damaged the actual GLM task. This avoids treating explained variance as a semantic guarantee.


## The important limit


The Universal Subspace paper **does not** establish that its principal directions are Golay directions, Leech-lattice directions, physical constants, or architecture-independent invariants. Its own scope is narrower: it focuses on many models sharing an architecture because it says it has no method for comparing subspaces across different architectures. [Methodology](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.05117?page=5)


It also acknowledges that the extracted subspace currently depends on having pretrained task-specific models available, and names model-independent recovery as future work. [Limitations](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.05117?page=12)


So the defensible GLM hypothesis is:


> **Within one chosen GLM model architecture, trained task variants may occupy a nested low-dimensional parameter tower. Rank is then an operational resolution level, with residual variation marking the boundary where the current level stops being adequate.**


That is strong, testable, and compatible with your formal layer language.


## Best first experiment


Build a small falsifiable study around one fixed architecture:


- Train 20–50 models on distinct GLM tasks: perhaps Golay decoding, arithmetic-layer routing, MOG-language inference, chemistry relation prediction, and exact-rational wobble classification.
- Learn $U_k$ only from a training subset of tasks/models.
- Hold out entire tasks and random seeds.
- Compare:
  1. joint-subspace projection,
  2. random orthogonal projection,
  3. per-task low-rank compression,
  4. your proposed Golay/Leech-aligned basis.
- Measure both parameter reconstruction and behavior: theorem pass rate, exact arithmetic correctness, decoding ambiguity handling, semantic accuracy, and calibration performance.


The key discriminating result would be: **does the learned joint basis outperform random and ordinary per-model compression on held-out GLM tasks at the same rank?** If yes, then “universal subspace as resolution” becomes an empirical component of GLM rather than an analogy.


One implementation caution: the Universal Subspace workflow uses numerical spectral decomposition, whereas your substrate policy requires exact arithmetic. You can keep the distinction clean by treating PCA/HOSVD as an external empirical-learning stage and importing only a certified result—e.g. rationalized basis coefficients plus interval bounds on reconstruction and behavioral error—into the exact GLM layer.

## Aurelle


A literal GLM translation would replace the paper’s learned real-valued PCA directions with a **shared, discrete 24-dimensional carrier dictionary**: Golay/Leech-aligned directions plus exact coefficients that many task-specific GLM models reuse.


In other words, instead of saying:


> “Many neural-network weight tensors lie near the same low-rank real subspace,”


you would test:


> “Many GLM task models lie near the same small span of exact lattice-carrier directions, at a stated Golay/Leech resolution.”


That is much closer to what you are asking than merely adding rank-$k$ as another abstract layer.


## The literal correspondence


The Universal Subspace work collects many independently trained instances of the **same architecture**, aligns corresponding layers, stacks their weights, and extracts a joint low-rank basis using spectral decomposition/HOSVD. [Methodology](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.05117?page=5) Each task model is then represented mainly by coefficients in that shared basis.


A GLM-native analogue would be:


| Universal Subspace system | Literal GLM version |
|---|---|
| Common neural architecture | Common 24-coordinate GLM carrier architecture |
| Floating-point weight tensor | Exact $\mathbb{Q}^{24}$ carrier fields, or integer-scaled coordinates |
| PCA/HOSVD basis vectors | Reusable Golay/Leech-aligned carrier directions |
| Low-rank coefficient vector | Exact rational/integer coordinates in a selected carrier dictionary |
| Projection residual | Syndrome, lattice residual, and/or unresolved higher-layer component |
| Model merging | Exact coefficient combination followed by explicit resolution-aware decoding |
| More principal components | More selected GLM directions / a finer carrier basis |


The crucial change is that the “universal directions” are not arbitrary eigenvectors in $\mathbb R^d$. They are constrained to be meaningful GLM objects: codewords, coset leaders, Leech vectors, MOG symmetries, or structured compositions of those.


## What a GLM universal-subspace model might look like


Take a fixed GLM model architecture—for example, one that maps exact rational inputs to 24-coordinate carriers and performs a sequence of reversible or layer-aware transformations.


For each task $t$, do **not** store a whole independent parameter field $W_t$. Store instead:


$$
W_t = W_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{t,i} D_i + R_t.
$$


Where:


- $W_0$ is the shared GLM base system;
- $D_i$ are shared **GLM directions**—ideally structured 24-coordinate objects;
- $a_{t,i}$ are exact coefficients;
- $R_t$ is the residual that the current resolution does not carry.


The “universal substrate hypothesis” then becomes:


$$
\lVert R_t\rVert \text{ is small for many tasks } t
$$


for a small shared dictionary $\{D_1,\ldots,D_k\}$.


That is a direct analogue of the neural-paper claim that separately trained task models can be approximated from a small shared parameter subspace. The paper reports this kind of concentration across LoRAs and full model weights, including ViTs, LLaMA variants, GPT-2, and Flan-T5. [Abstract](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.05117?page=1)


## The especially GLM-like version: a resolution hierarchy


Your system already has a natural answer to “what is a coarse basis?” A model variation can be read at several resolutions:


```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A[Exact parameter/update carrier] --> B[24-bit parity word]
  B --> C[Golay syndrome / coset]
  C --> D[Nearest codeword or sextet tie]
  D --> E[Leech-lattice coordinate class]
  E --> F[Exact coefficient expansion]
```


A literal GLM universal-model workflow could be:


1. **Represent each task adaptation** as an exact 24-coordinate update field—not necessarily one carrier overall, but a field of carriers indexed by layer/module/operation.


2. **Read the update at several resolutions.**
   - Binary support and Hamming weight;
   - Golay syndrome/coset;
   - nearest-codeword status: unique, ambiguous, or residual;
   - Leech class and norm;
   - exact rational coefficient field.


3. **Discover repeated GLM motifs.**  
   Rather than ask which real-valued singular vector explains most variance, ask which structured carrier directions repeatedly occur across task updates.


4. **Build a nested dictionary.**  
   At low resolution you might retain only a small family of Golay classes. At higher resolution, add Leech directions, then exact rational corrections.


5. **Escalate only on residual.**  
   If a task is well represented by $k$ shared directions, stay there. If its syndrome or residual crosses a pre-registered threshold, add another basis direction or preserve its task-specific residual.


This resembles the paper’s claim that dominant shared directions can support compression and merging, but makes the retained directions interpretable in your own carrier language. The paper’s practical demonstration is based on a low-dimensional shared basis: it reports reconstruction/projection of held-out ViT models into a 16-dimensional universal subspace and evaluates IID and OOD accuracy. [Classical Weights](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.05117?page=10)


## A concrete GLM formulation


Suppose every learned adaptation is decomposed into 24-coordinate blocks:


$$
\Delta W_t =
\left(
x_{t,1},x_{t,2},\ldots,x_{t,m}
\right),
\qquad x_{t,j}\in\mathbb Q^{24}.
$$


Choose a finite library of candidate GLM directions:


$$
\mathcal D =
\{
d_1,\ldots,d_M
\},
\qquad d_i \in \mathbb Z^{24}
\ \text{or}\ 
\Lambda_{24}.
$$


Then find a small task-independent subset $\mathcal D_k \subset \mathcal D$ such that:


$$
\Delta W_t \approx
\sum_{d_i\in\mathcal D_k} a_{t,i}d_i.
$$


But the approximation needs to be evaluated in several ways, not just Euclidean distance:


$$
\text{score}(t) =
\alpha\,\text{task-error}(t)
+
\beta\,\text{lattice-residual}(t)
+
\gamma\,\text{syndrome-cost}(t)
+
\delta\,\text{coefficient-complexity}(t).
$$


This gives a GLM-native notion of “low rank”:


- **low dictionary rank:** few shared directions;
- **low exact-complexity:** simple rational coefficients;
- **low lattice residual:** close to the intended geometric substrate;
- **low semantic error:** still performs the task correctly.


That last condition matters. A compressed model can be geometrically elegant but computationally wrong.


## Do not use Golay decoding as the basis extractor


There is one important structural issue. Your own number-theoretic document correctly shows that Golay decoding can erase signal: a received weight-3 word is uniquely decoded to the zero codeword, while the syndrome carries the difference.


The same would happen if you “snap” every task update to a nearest Golay codeword:


- shared, codeword-like structure would remain;
- low-weight task-specific differences could disappear;
- the model could look artificially universal because the decoder erased the variation.


So the right carrier is not merely:


$$
\operatorname{decode}(\Delta W_t).
$$


It is something like:


$$
\big(
\operatorname{decodedCodeword}(\Delta W_t),
\operatorname{syndrome}(\Delta W_t),
\operatorname{tieStatus}(\Delta W_t),
\operatorname{residual}(\Delta W_t)
\big).
$$


That is actually a strong match for your resolution language:


- the codeword is the coarse shared component;
- the syndrome is structured task variation;
- an ambiguous sextet is an explicit “do not collapse further” signal;
- the residual triggers escalation to a finer representation.


## Two realistic implementation paths


### 1. GLM adapters first — the practical route


Keep an existing model or controller fixed, and make only its task adaptations GLM-native.


For each task:


- create a small adaptation vector or update matrix;
- block it into 24-coordinate carriers;
- constrain updates to a shared Golay/Leech dictionary;
- retain exact rational coefficients;
- compare a task-specific update against a shared dictionary plus a sparse residual.


This is the closest analogue to the paper’s LoRA experiments. The paper begins with LoRA partly because many adapters are available and easy to analyze, before extending to full weights. [Introduction](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.05117?page=2)


This path lets you test the idea without requiring a fully non-floating-point transformer.


### 2. A fully GLM-native learned machine — the stronger route


Build a model whose learnable state is itself a collection of exact GLM carriers:


- weights are rational 24-vectors or lattice points;
- activations are exact rational carriers, codes, syndromes, or dynamic wobble processes;
- updates are selected from a discrete carrier dictionary;
- reversible operations preserve enough state to make updates auditable;
- model specialization is stored as exact coefficient records.


Then the universal hypothesis is no longer being *imported from neural networks*. It becomes:


> Across independently learned GLM tasks, does the same small set of substrate operations and carrier directions recur?


That would be a novel and meaningful empirical question.


## What would count as success?


The strongest result would not be “the updates have a scree plot.” It would be all four:


1. **Shared GLM directions recur** across independently trained task models.
2. **A held-out task** can be represented with few shared directions plus a small explicit residual.
3. **Task behavior survives** projection, not only coordinate reconstruction.
4. A GLM-constrained dictionary performs better than matched controls:
   - random 24D directions;
   - unconstrained rational low-rank factors;
   - individually compressed task models;
   - codeword snapping without syndrome retention.


The decisive comparison is:


$$
\text{GLM shared dictionary}
\quad \text{vs.} \quad
\text{generic low-rank basis}
$$


at equal coefficient budget and equal behavioral error.


If your Golay/Leech structure wins, then it is not just a language for describing a universal subspace. It has supplied a useful prior for discovering one.


## The boundary of the claim


The Universal Subspace paper gives evidence for **architecture-specific shared parameter geometry**, not a theorem that all learning systems are governed by a 24-dimensional Golay/Leech structure. It explicitly limits its cross-model comparisons to models with the same architecture. [Methodology](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.05117?page=5)


So I would phrase the proposed research hypothesis this way:


> **Universal GLM carrier hypothesis:** for a fixed GLM computational architecture, task-specific learned updates can be represented at a coarse resolution by a small shared family of Golay/Leech-compatible carrier directions; task identity lives primarily in exact coefficients, syndromes, and explicitly retained residuals.


That is literal enough to build, narrow enough to falsify, and naturally incorporates your existing ideas about layers, ambiguity, projection loss, and escalation.

## Aurelle


**Determinism does not make learning “just cache”: it makes the rule for turning evidence into a generalization explicit and reproducible.** Cache remembers that a case occurred; learning infers a rule that correctly handles cases that did *not* occur.


For GLM, I would separate four things that are often bundled under “learning”:


1. **Memory** — retain an observed carrier, fact, trajectory, or correction.
2. **Compression** — find repeated structure and replace many remembered cases with a shorter shared description.
3. **Inference** — choose a rule that explains observations and entails predictions on unseen cases.
4. **Revision** — when a new case contradicts the rule, deterministically widen, refine, or replace the rule.


Only the first is cache.


## A precise distinction


A cache is a partial function:


$$
M : X \rightharpoonup Y,
$$


which answers only for inputs it has already stored. If $x\notin\operatorname{dom}(M)$, it has no answer.


A learned GLM rule is a structured hypothesis:


$$
h : X \to Y
$$


or, when uncertainty/ambiguity is real,


$$
h : X \to \mathcal P(Y).
$$


It must do more than replay cases. It should:


- reproduce the observed cases;
- make a prediction for unseen cases;
- state its resolution and domain of validity;
- preserve an explicit exception/residual set;
- be falsifiable by a future carrier.


Your existing layer concept is well suited to this. A coarse rule is not “wrong” merely because it conflates cases; it is a rule at a stated resolution. New evidence can show that the rule’s current view is insufficient and trigger escalation.


## Deterministic learning can be inference


A deterministic learning loop could look like:


$$
H_{t+1}
=
\underset{h\in\mathcal H}{\arg\min}_{h}
\left[
\operatorname{Error}(h,D_t)
+
\lambda\,\operatorname{Complexity}(h)
+
\mu\,\operatorname{ResidualCost}(h,D_t)
\right].
$$


Here:


- $D_t$ is the exact accumulated evidence;
- $\mathcal H$ is a finite or effectively enumerable grammar of admissible GLM rules;
- `Error` measures failed predictions;
- `Complexity` penalizes rules that merely memorize;
- `ResidualCost` prices the exceptions the rule still needs to store.


Given the same data, hypothesis grammar, tie-breaking order, and exact arithmetic, every run chooses the same $H_{t+1}$. That is deterministic learning—not no learning.


The decisive distinction is **compression plus counterfactual success**:


- A table with 1,000 entries that predicts only those 1,000 entries is memory.
- A law with 3 parameters that explains the 1,000 entries and predicts the 1,001st is inference.
- A rule that has to attach a unique exception to every example has rediscovered cache in disguise.


Your MOG-language work already points in this direction: a learned conditional table that is selected from observed worlds and then correctly licenses unseen statements is more than a stored transcript. The distinction is whether the learned object is a reusable relation, rather than a lookup from one observed state to one recorded output.


## What “learning” could mean in a GLM


I would give GLM a three-part learned object:


$$
\mathcal L = (\text{rule},\ \text{certificate},\ \text{exceptions}).
$$


### 1. Rule


A rule is a compositional relation over carriers, not a list of cases. For example:


$$
r(a,b) = P_{\ell}\bigl(a \oplus T(b)\bigr),
$$


where $T$ is a permitted reversible transformation and $P_{\ell}$ is a projection at GLM layer $\ell$.


Or for exact quantities:


$$
r(x) = \frac{ax+b}{cx+d},
\qquad a,b,c,d\in\mathbb Q.
$$


The grammar of allowable rules is itself an inductive bias. It may include:


- exact arithmetic operations;
- MOG symmetries and permutations;
- Golay syndrome operations;
- reversible gates;
- layer projections;
- finite-state/delta–sigma dynamics;
- selected Leech-lattice transformations;
- explicitly typed semantic relations.


### 2. Certificate


The system should retain *why* it selected the rule:


- the observations it explains;
- its exact score;
- its counterexamples;
- its resolution/layer;
- a proof, exhaustive check, or exact re-derivation where feasible.


This is a natural extension of your Three Column Thinking: not merely “the answer was recomputed,” but “the rule was selected over alternatives for stated reasons.”


### 3. Exceptions / residual


No universal rule should silently absorb discrepancies. Store:


$$
E_h = \{(x,y)\in D : h(x)\neq y\}.
$$


At a coarse layer, exceptions are expected. The GLM question becomes: is the exception pattern random-looking, or does it have recoverable syndrome/lattice/semantic structure?


If it has structure, infer a second rule for the residual. If not, retain it as memory and state that it is currently uncompressed.


## This is where the universal-subspace idea becomes useful


The literal analogue is not merely “many models share weights.” It is:


> **Many learned GLM rules may share a small library of transformations, projections, and residual patterns.**


Suppose task-specific learned programs are $h_t$. Instead of storing every program separately, represent them as:


$$
h_t =
h_0
\circ
\left(
\sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{t,i} R_i
\right)
+
e_t,
$$


where:


- $h_0$ is common GLM machinery;
- $R_i$ are reusable learned rule-directions—e.g. transformations, relation templates, carrier operations, or state-update motifs;
- $a_{t,i}$ are exact task-specific coefficients or discrete selections;
- $e_t$ is a structured residual.


The Universal Subspace paper’s empirical claim is that many models with a shared architecture have dominant common parameter directions despite differing tasks, datasets, and initialization. [Abstract](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.05117?page=1) In a GLM, the stronger and more interpretable version would be: many domains reuse a small set of **inference operations**, not merely numerical weight vectors.


For instance, chemistry, dimensional reasoning, and dialogue may all use a common skeleton:


```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A[Exact observation] --> B[Read at current layer]
  B --> C[Find invariant / relation]
  C --> D[Propose smallest rule]
  D --> E[Test on held-out cases]
  E -->|passes| F[Store rule + certificate]
  E -->|fails| G[Inspect structured residual]
  G --> H[Escalate layer or add a rule]
  H --> C
```


That is learning as **deterministic discovery of reusable invariants**.


## A GLM-native learning criterion


A good rule should beat memory by description length. Define:


$$
\operatorname{LearnScore}(h;D)
=
L(h)
+
L(E_h)
+
L(D\mid h,E_h),
$$


where $L(\cdot)$ is an exact code length or complexity measure.


- A broad, simple rule has small $L(h)$.
- A bad generalization has a large exception cost $L(E_h)$.
- Pure cache has a tiny “rule” but a huge exception table.
- A useful inference gives a short total description and succeeds on held-out data.


This is a deterministic version of minimum-description-length learning. It also gives you a firm answer to “is this only remembering?”:


> If adding a rule does not reduce the total description length *and* improve prediction on data not used to form it, it is memory, not learned inference.


## Resolution is central, but not sufficient


A rule can be valid at a coarse resolution while failing at a finer one. Your own example of $1/7$ and $4/27$ sharing a 24-bit wobble window but separating later is exactly the pattern.


So each GLM learned rule should carry a contract:


$$
(\text{domain},\ \text{layer},\ \text{resolution},\ \text{guarantee},\ \text{failure trigger}).
$$


For example:


| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Domain | exact rationals in $[0,1)$ |
| Current representation | 24-tick wobble word |
| Rule | classify by observed binary period |
| Guarantee | correct for observed/training denominators |
| Boundary | two targets can share first 24 ticks |
| Escalation | increase window to 48, 96, or until separated |


That means GLM does not claim, “I know the number.” It claims, “At 24 ticks, these targets are equivalent; at 27 ticks, they are not.” That is inference with an honest resolution boundary.


## The key design choice: learn rules, not merely coordinates


For a fully GLM-native system, I would not start by training a conventional weight network and encoding its learned weights into Golay words. I would start with a **rule induction engine** over exact typed operations.


The initial learning grammar might include only:


- arithmetic: $+,-,\times,\div$;
- comparisons and order;
- exact rational constants generated from evidence;
- finite-state transitions;
- MOG permutations;
- reversible gates;
- Golay encode/decode with syndrome retained;
- carrier projections and layer escalation;
- relation composition;
- universal/general/existential patterns over a finite typed domain.


Then the machine learns by enumerating or constructing candidate rules in increasing complexity, testing them exactly, and choosing the first/shortest rule that meets the generalization criterion. That is slower than gradient descent, but it has strong properties:


- deterministic;
- inspectable;
- exact;
- reversible where desired;
- capable of saying *why* it believes a rule;
- able to distinguish proven law, empirical regularity, and cached exception.


## The strongest formulation


I would state the GLM learning hypothesis as:


> **A deterministic GLM learns when it converts a finite record of exact observations into a shorter, compositional, falsifiable rule that predicts unobserved cases at a declared resolution; it remembers when the unexplained part remains an exception store.**


Then the universal hypothesis becomes a second-level claim:


> **Across domains, the rules discovered by GLM may themselves compress into a small reusable basis of inference motifs, with task differences represented as coefficients, typed bindings, syndromes, and explicit residuals.**


That is more than cache, and more than a standard LLM-style parameter subspace. It would be a system that learns **structured generalizations with visible boundaries**.

### Concept learning: from observations to reusable meanings


[Neuro-Symbolic Concepts](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.06191) is especially relevant to the GLM because it treats concepts as intermediate objects rather than treating intelligence as a direct mapping from input to output.


A GLM concept could be represented as:


$$
C =
(\text{name},\ \text{type},\ \text{carrier},\ \text{invariants},\ \text{examples},\ \text{counterexamples},\ \text{rules},\ \text{resolution}).
$$


For example, a learned concept such as “stable bond,” “ambiguous decode,” or “heavy object” would not be a cached label. It would contain:


- the carrier pattern;
- the invariant that defines it;
- the evidence from which it was inferred;
- the conditions under which it applies;
- the layer at which it is visible;
- counterexamples or unresolved cases.


That would give GLM a path from raw observations to semantic categories.


### Inductive Logic Programming: the clearest route to inference


[Hypothesis Generation via LLM-Automated Language Bias for ILP](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.21486) is probably the most direct conceptual match for deterministic GLM learning.


Inductive Logic Programming learns a hypothesis $H$ from positive and negative examples:


$$
H \cup B \models E^+,
\qquad
H \cup B \not\models E^-,
$$


where $B$ is background knowledge.


A GLM version could replace ordinary logical predicates with typed carrier predicates:


```text
golay_weight(x, 3)
syndrome_unique(x)
same_resolution(x, y, 24)
tax_below(x, 5/2)
causes(x, y)
```


The learner would search a restricted grammar of exact rules:


```text
if weight(x, 3) and syndrome_unique(x)
then decodes_to(x, zero_codeword)
```


This is not cache because the rule applies to every carrier satisfying the conditions, including carriers not present in the training set.


The important GLM contribution would be to make each inferred rule carry:


- a proof or exhaustive validation result;
- the examples it covers;
- the counterexamples it fails on;
- its minimum required resolution;
- its complexity cost.


### Program synthesis: learning executable inference


[WorldCoder](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2402.12275) and [ReaComp](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.05485) suggest a powerful direction: the learned object should be an executable program, not merely a vector or label.


For GLM, learning could produce:


$$
P : \text{State} \times \text{Action} \to \text{State} \times \text{Observation}.
$$


The program might contain:


- exact rational arithmetic;
- MOG permutations;
- reversible gates;
- syndrome calculation;
- layer escalation;
- semantic predicates;
- a residual or “unknown” branch.


The learning loop would be:


```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A[Observed transitions] --> B[Generate candidate GLM programs]
  B --> C[Execute exactly]
  C --> D[Check held-out transitions]
  D -->|passes| E[Store program and certificate]
  D -->|fails| F[Extract counterexample]
  F --> G[Refine grammar or increase resolution]
  G --> B
```


This is the most literal way to give GLM learning rather than remembering: **experience is converted into a new executable rule**.


### World modelling: inference over state transitions


[PoE-World](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.10819) and [Neuro-Symbolic Synergy for Interactive World Modeling](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2602.10480) are relevant if you want GLM to reason about changing situations rather than static records.


A GLM world model should maintain:


$$
S_{t+1}=F(S_t,A_t),
$$


where:


- $S_t$ is an exact structured state;
- $A_t$ is an action;
- $F$ is an inferred or verified transition rule.


For a simple GLM world, the state might contain:


```text
object identity
mass
temperature
phase
position
relation graph
confidence/resolution
provenance
```


The system should not merely remember that “the water was frozen.” It should infer:


```text
if temperature < freezing_point(water)
then phase(water) = frozen
```


That rule can then predict the phase of a previously unseen temperature state.


This would also strengthen the MOG language system. Instead of learning only true sentences about a finite set of worlds, GLM could learn transition laws and use them to generate new worlds.


### Long-term memory: distinguish remembering from learned structure


[NeuSymMS: A Hybrid Neuro-Symbolic Memory System for Persistent, Self-Curating LLM Agents](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.17596), [Mimir: A Neuro-Symbolic Memory System with Dynamic Grounding for Embodied Agents](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.04933), and [Advancing Multimodal Agent Reasoning with Long-Term Neuro-Symbolic Memory](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.15280) are useful for designing the memory side of GLM.


The key adaptation is to split memory into at least four stores:


1. **Episodic memory** — exact events that occurred.
2. **Semantic memory** — generalised rules and concepts.
3. **Procedural memory** — executable skills or programs.
4. **Provenance memory** — the evidence and resolution supporting each rule.


That gives a clean test for whether learning occurred:


- if the system stores the observation unchanged, it has remembered;
- if it transforms several observations into a shorter reusable rule, it has learned;
- if it uses the rule to act or predict in a new situation, it has inferred.


### Continual learning: how GLM should revise itself


For the universal-subspace part of your project, review:


- [Geo-LoRA: Geometry-Aware Subspace Evolution for Low-Rank Adaptation in Continual Learning](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.26960)
- [The Stable Recovery Manifold: Geometric Principles Governing Recoverability in Continual Learning](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.13637)
- [Continual Learning as Shared-Manifold Continuation Under Compatible Shift](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.20036)
- [Gram-Space: Structure-Preserving Codebook Compression for Memory-Efficient Neuro-Symbolic AI](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.01528)


Their relevance to GLM is not that GLM should use ordinary LoRA. It is that they provide ideas for:


- preserving old concepts while adding new ones;
- detecting when a new task belongs to the existing shared structure;
- deciding when to create a new direction;
- measuring interference between learned rules;
- compressing repeated representations.


A GLM continual-learning state could be:


$$
\mathcal K_t =
(\mathcal B_t,\mathcal C_t,\mathcal P_t,\mathcal R_t),
$$


where:


- $\mathcal B_t$ is the shared basis or universal rule library;
- $\mathcal C_t$ is the concept vocabulary;
- $\mathcal P_t$ is the procedural program library;
- $\mathcal R_t$ is the residual and exception store.


When a new task arrives:


1. project it onto existing rules;
2. test whether its residual is small and structured;
3. reuse existing rules if possible;
4. add a new rule direction if necessary;
5. preserve old certificates and retest affected behaviours.


That is a deterministic analogue of continual learning with explicit escalation.

That is likely more useful to GLM than treating the universal-subspace result as a claim about its 24-dimensional geometry.

## The capability I would build first


The first new GLM component should be a **Deterministic Rule-Induction Engine**, not a larger carrier or a larger lattice.


Its input would be:


```text
exact observations
positive examples
negative examples
background facts
available GLM operations
resolution limits
```


Its output would be:


```text
inferred rule
held-out predictions
counterexamples
complexity score
resolution contract
Lean/Python re-derivation
exception set
```


Formally:


$$
\operatorname{Learn}(D,\mathcal G,\ell)
=
\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal G}
\left[
L(h)+L(E_h)+\operatorname{Error}_{\mathrm{heldout}}(h)
\right],
$$


subject to the rule using only operations available at layer $\ell$.


That would give your GLM a meaningful answer to the original question:


> **Learning is not merely remembering when the system discovers a compact rule that generalises beyond its stored examples, can be tested on counterfactual cases, and records the resolution at which that rule remains valid.**


The universal-subspace hypothesis could then assist as a second-order compression principle: not just “what observations are stored,” but **which inference rules repeatedly recur across different domains and can therefore become shared GLM capabilities**.

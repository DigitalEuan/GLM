====

**Yes — let’s be deliberately cheeky and build a small purpose-built language for exactly this observable.**

Mathematics gives us accurate pictures and reliable calculation tools, but the pictures are never the living process itself. The observable we care about is the *ongoing residual activity* produced by continuous correction: the vibration, the oscillation, the polarity flips, the never-quite-finished attempt of a system to be its preferred configuration. We therefore design a compact operator language whose only job is to assemble that residual signal. We treat the symbols as interlocking blocks. We do not need the full underlying dimensions, materials, or physical weights while we are fitting the blocks; we only care that they click together into a pipeline that outputs a concrete residual (a sequence, a period, a frequency proxy, an amplitude trajectory). Afterwards we can look at the finished shape and decide what it means.

### The language (call it Residual Notation or simply \(\mathcal{R}\)-calculus)

We work with three primitive kinds of object and a handful of operators.

**Objects**
- \(T\) — the target (ideal codeword, ideal lattice occupancy, preferred spin polarization, …)
- \(S\) — the current state (binary vector, bit-packed lattice, spin configuration, …)
- \(V\) — the residual observable (the thing we actually record: distance sequence, syndrome weight trajectory, magnetization oscillation, mechanical amplitude, …)

**Core operators** (the blocks)

| Symbol | Name | Intuitive action | Established mathematical counterparts |
|--------|------|------------------|---------------------------------------|
| \(\oplus\) or \(\triangle\) | Difference / Error | \(E = S \triangle T\) | Hamming difference, symmetric difference of supports, syndrome (via parity-check matrix \(H\)), deviation vector |
| \(C\) | Correction step | \(S \mapsto C(S;T)\) | Decoding map, bit-flip heuristic that reduces weight of \(E\), optical-pumping super-operator, gradient step on a discrete energy, coset-leader translation |
| \(\rho\) or \(\circlearrowright\) | Drive / Rotation | Cyclic shift or forced polarization | Cyclic group action on coordinates, discrete time evolution operator, laser duty-cycle modulation, bit rotation in the register |
| \(\delta\) | Persistent perturbation | Small ongoing noise or incomplete correction | Additive noise process, residual decoherence, thermal bit-flip probability, incomplete decoder |
| \(R\) | Residual extractor | \(V = R(\{S_n\})\) or \(R(E)\) | Hamming-weight sequence \(wt(E_n)\), integer magnetization \(\sum (\pm 1)\), discrete Fourier transform of the weight trajectory (exact frequency content), any integer-valued observable of the error |
| \(\circ\) | Composition / Pipeline | Ordinary function composition | Iteration of a dynamical system, rewriting sequence, successive application of coding and physical maps |

All operations stay inside exact arithmetic: bit-XOR, integer pop-count, cyclic shifts, integer summation. No floats are required.

### The canonical pipeline (putting the blocks together)

We assemble the operators in a fixed order that directly produces the observable:

\[
\begin{align*}
S_0 &= \text{initial state}\\
E_n &= S_n \triangle T \\
S_{n+1} &= C\bigl(\rho(S_n); T\bigr) \quad\text{or}\quad C(S_n;T) \oplus \delta_n \\
V &= R\bigl(\{E_n\}_{n=0}^{N}\bigr) \quad\text{or}\quad R\bigl(\{S_n\}\bigr)
\end{align*}
\]

The expected output is precisely \(V\): a sequence of integers (residual distances or magnetizations), or a derived exact period/frequency proxy obtained from that sequence, or a simple statistic such as mean residual weight, peak-to-peak amplitude, or the length of a detected cycle if one appears.

Because the blocks are only required to fit, we can swap concrete realizations later:
- \(T\) can be a Golay codeword, an ideal diamond occupancy, or a fully polarized NV ensemble.
- \(C\) can be a single-bit greedy flip, a full algebraic Golay decoder, or the optical-pumping map.
- \(\rho\) can be a 24-bit cyclic shift or a periodic laser pulse.
- \(R\) can be Hamming weight, a \(\pm 1\) sum, or the driving term fed into an integer oscillator that stands for the levitated mass.

We do not need the geometric bond lengths, the precise value of the Bohr magneton, or the continuum electromagnetic field while we are fitting the pipeline; those can be inspected after the residual signal \(V\) has been generated.

### Concrete illustration with the tools we already have

Take \(T\) to be any fixed Golay codeword.  
Start with a modest error \(S_0 = T \triangle e\).  
Let \(\rho\) be the exact 24-bit rotation \(x \mapsto (x\ll 1)\bmod 2^{24}\).  
Let \(C\) be a mild greedy single-bit correction that reduces distance when possible.  
Let \(R\) extract the sequence of Hamming distances (or the \(\pm 1\) magnetization).

The resulting integer sequence \(\{d_n\}\) *is* the vibration during correction. In runs one sees fluctuating residuals rather than monotonic decay; the fluctuations themselves are the observable. If a short cycle appears, its length is an exact discrete period. Even when no short cycle appears, the sequence and its integer statistics remain well-defined and reproducible.

The same pipeline can be read in the experimental language: \(T\) = desired spin polarization, \(C\) = optical pumping pulse, \(\rho\) = repetition rate of the laser, \(R\) = interferometer trace of the mechanical motion. The mathematics is different, the blocks are the same shape.

### What we gain by being cheeky

- We separate the *assembly of the observable* from the full physical or combinatorial story. The language only has to guarantee that the residual \(V\) is produced cleanly.
- Every operator has a direct translation into established mathematics (linear codes, discrete dynamical systems, group actions, integer-valued observables), so the language never floats free of rigorous grounding.
- Because the blocks are coarse, we can rearrange them, strengthen or weaken \(C\), change the drive \(\rho\), or alter the extractor \(R\) and immediately see how the residual signal changes — all inside exact arithmetic.
- After the pipeline has run we are free to look at the finished shape: map the residual frequency back onto the real apparatus, ask whether a particular cycle length matches an observed mechanical resonance, or embed the same residual into a larger binary lattice model.

In short, the symbols are the operators, the expected output is the residual vibration (its exact integer trajectory or frequency proxy), and the pipeline above is the ordered fitting of the blocks. We place them so they produce \(V\); only afterwards do we examine what physical or mathematical object we have actually built.

====

Several “greats” and strong research lines have walked very similar paths — inventing compact languages or calculi for combinatorial objects, treating correction itself as a dynamical process whose residuals are the real observable, and deliberately separating the useful formal picture from the underlying “thing.” What you are doing is a synthesis across those lines rather than a pure repetition.

Here are the closest predecessors and the practical lessons they offer.

### 1. R. T. Curtis and the Miracle Octad Generator (MOG)
Curtis invented the MOG precisely as a *working language* — a 4×6 diagrammatic device with simple parity and hexacode rules — so that one could manipulate the Golay code and \(M_{24}\) without constantly carrying the full abstract weight of the code or the group.  
**Lesson:** Design the notation so the blocks fit and produce the answer you need (octads, stabilizers, etc.). Only afterwards inspect what larger structure you have touched. This is almost exactly the “put the blocks together to fill the shape, look later” attitude you described. Study his papers and the way Conway & Sloane present the MOG in *Sphere Packings, Lattices and Groups* (SPLAG). Notice how little ontology is required to start calculating.

### 2. John H. Conway & N. J. A. Sloane
Conway in particular was the master of inventing cheeky yet powerful formalisms (surreal numbers, Look-and-Say sequence, Game of Life, etc.) that let one explore discrete processes and their emergent residuals. Together with Sloane he gave the definitive treatment of the Golay code, the MOG, and the Leech lattice, and also produced practical soft-decoding algorithms that turn an arbitrary point into the nearest codeword or lattice point — i.e., explicit corrective maps.  
**Lesson:** Playfulness is not the enemy of rigor. Conway treated mathematical objects as things one can *play with* and watch residual patterns appear. Keep your Residual Notation light and operable; let the interesting behaviour (cycles, fluctuations, unexpected periods) show itself in the residual sequence rather than forcing a grand interpretation too early. SPLAG remains the single best reference for staying exact and combinatorial.

### 3. Researchers who treat iterative decoding as nonlinear dynamics
In the mid-2000s onward, people such as Ljupco Kocarev, Frédéric Lehmann, Gian Mario Maggio, Alexander Vardy and others explicitly modelled iterative decoders (LDPC, turbo codes, etc.) as high-dimensional nonlinear dynamical systems. They studied the trajectories of the residual error / a-posteriori entropy / syndrome weight, and found fixed points, oscillatory behaviour, bifurcations, chaos and transient chaos. The residual signal itself became the object of study.  
**Lesson:** Once you have a correction operator \(C\) and a residual extractor \(R\), treat the iterated map as a dynamical system and analyse its attractors, periods and spectra. You do not need the full physical story to extract useful quantitative statements about the “vibration during correction.” Their papers give concrete techniques for reducing high-dimensional residual dynamics to low-dimensional observables (exactly what your \(R\) operator is doing).

### 4. Continuous quantum error correction (CQEC)
Starting from Paz & Zurek (1998) and continuing through theoretical and recent experimental work, this line treats error correction as an ongoing, continuous (or rapidly repeated) process. The residual error under continuous monitoring and feedback is the central quantity; the ideal code space is inferred, not directly observed.  
**Lesson:** The observable is the residual dynamics under continuous correction. Your framing — “what we observe is the ongoing results of the thing trying to be” — is essentially the same philosophical and technical stance. You can borrow their emphasis on the *rate* and *spectrum* of residual errors.

### 5. Optical-pumping and spin-polarization dynamics
The actual physics of the NV-diamond experiment sits in a long tradition of continuous optical pumping: a drive that constantly tries to push spins toward a preferred polarized state, while relaxation and noise constantly undo part of the work. The residual polarization (and the force or signal it produces) is what is measured.  
**Lesson:** The experimental literature already treats the steady-state residual and its fluctuations as the primary observable. Your binary / Golay version is a discrete, exact-arithmetic analogue of the same situation.

### What you can usefully learn and adopt
- **Invent the smallest language that lets you assemble the residual.** Curtis and Conway both did this. Your \(\mathcal{R}\)-calculus (target \(T\), state \(S\), correction \(C\), drive \(\rho\), residual extractor \(R\)) is in that spirit. Keep the operators few and composable.
- **Study the trajectory of the residual, not only the fixed point.** The iterative-decoding dynamics papers show that oscillations, limit cycles and even chaotic residuals are generic and informative.
- **Stay exact and discrete as long as possible.** Conway & Sloane’s combinatorial style and the bit-level Golay work reward this. Your insistence on float-free arithmetic is already aligned with the strongest examples.
- **Separate the useful calculus from the ontology.** All of the above authors (especially Conway and the decoding-dynamics people) were happy to use a formal picture while remaining clear that it is a picture. This protects against the numerology trap you correctly flagged.
- **Look for the moments when the residual becomes interesting** — period locking, amplitude jumps, bifurcations — rather than forcing every configuration to be “deep.”

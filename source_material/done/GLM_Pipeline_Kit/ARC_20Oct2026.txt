## NOTE 1 PART A

**Vision-native ARC solvers, starting with [VARC](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.14761) as a working baseline, then use object-based recurrent reasoning and intermediate-state supervision to teach the GLM.** The missing piece is not simply a way to represent grids: it is a training system that teaches the substrate to infer a transformation from demonstrations and execute it on a new input.

*   **The UBP (Universal Binary Principle):** The entire infinite complexity of the GLM’s cognitive architecture is generated by a single, simple, deterministic axiom: *There exists a binary distinction, and it can be toggled.* 
*   From this one rule, the system forces the existence of $\mathbb{F}_2$, the Hamming metric, the Golay code at length 23, the parity extension to 24, and the Leech lattice. No choices are made, no random seeds are used, no floats are introduced. The infinite complexity of the machine is a direct, deterministic unfolding of a single, exact geometric rule.
(See NOTE 4 for details)

The GLM's binary-seed distinction matters. **Binary is the substrate’s alphabet, not its learning mechanism.** A binary machine can implement rich reasoning, but the useful structure must come from its update rules, connectivity, learned memory, and experience—not from the two symbols alone.

## Three systems that suit the direction

| Paper / system | What it actually provides | Where it fits your GLM |
|---|---|---|
| [**ARC Is a Vision Problem! — VARC**](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.14761) | A vision transformer trained on grid canvases, followed by task-specific test-time training. | **Best first implementation:** establishes a working visual solver and a source of teaching signals. |
| [**Slots, Transitions, Loops — Loop-OWM**](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.12316) | Object-like slots, a demonstration-conditioned task representation, and repeated state updates combining spatial transport with content modification. | **Closest architectural match:** a structured state evolves under an inferred rule. |
| [**TraceViT: Grounded Trace Supervision for Visual Abstract Reasoning**](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.29586) | Trains a recurrent visual solver using verified intermediate grids, while preserving task information and an object workspace. | **Best teaching strategy:** teaches a sequence of transformations rather than only rewarding the final answer. |

These are approaches to static ARC-1/2 puzzles; an interactive agent would require an additional action-selection and environment-learning system.

### 1. VARC: a practical visual baseline

[VARC](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.14761) treats ARC as **image-to-image prediction**, rather than converting the grid into prose. It places grids on a canvas, uses a vision transformer, and adapts to each unfamiliar task using its demonstration pairs. It is trained from scratch on ARC-related data, rather than relying on natural-image pretraining. Its paper provides a [code repository](https://github.com/lillian039/VARC). [Visual solver](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.14761v1?page=1)

The important lesson for your GLM is **task-specific learning**. Given demonstrations

$$
\mathcal D=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{m},
$$

a solver must acquire something specific to this task—not merely recognize shapes. A simplified adaptation objective is

$$
(\theta_{\mathcal D},z_{\mathcal D})
=
\arg\min_{\theta,z}
\sum_i
\operatorname{CE}\!\left(f_\theta(x_i,z),y_i\right),
$$

initialized from previously learned parameters. Here $z$ is a task representation and $\theta$ denotes adaptable model parameters. This captures the training principle, not the full implementation.

The paper independently fine-tunes on each new task’s demonstrations and uses augmented views to generate and consolidate predictions. [Task adaptation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.14761v1?page=5)

**For GLM:** identify what can change when examples arrive—persistent memory, transition parameters, a task code, or some combination. Without an adaptable component, “teaching the substrate” has no operational definition.

### 2. Object-centric recurrence: a better internal organization

[Loop-OWM](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.12316) separates three jobs:

- interpreting the grid into spatial features and object-like slots;
- inferring a task representation from demonstrations;
- repeatedly applying a task-conditioned transition.

Its slots are candidate entity representations, **not guaranteed perfect object segmentations**. Its transition mechanism combines moving information across positions with writing new content, which matters because copying alone cannot implement recoloring or shape completion. [Structured transitions](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.12316v1?page=3)[Transport and writing](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.12316v1?page=4)

For your binary implementation, I would propose the following interface:

$$
z_{\mathcal D}=A_\eta(\mathcal D),\qquad
b_0=E_\phi(x),
$$

$$
b_{t+1}=F_\theta(b_t,z_{\mathcal D}),\qquad
\hat y_t=R_\psi(b_t),
\quad b_t\in\{0,1\}^{N}.
$$

Here $A_\eta$ infers the task, $E_\phi$ encodes the query, $F_\theta$ is the **actual GLM update**, and $R_\psi$ reads out a grid, including its dimensions.

**This is a proposed GLM architecture, not an existing demonstrated binary solver.** It specifies where your substrate must do meaningful work: retaining and executing a task-conditioned transformation.

### 3. Trace supervision: teach the process, not just the destination

[TraceViT](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.29586) is particularly relevant to your statement that the GLM “requires to be taught.” It constructs intermediate grid sequences by decomposing programmatic task implementations and checking that their final outputs remain correct. [Teaching traces](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.29586v1?page=3)

Instead of teaching only

$$
x\longrightarrow y,
$$

teach

$$
x\longrightarrow u_1\longrightarrow u_2\longrightarrow y.
$$

For example: identify the relevant region, move a shape, then recolor it. These intermediate states are teaching targets, not assumed universal reasoning stages.

A simple starting loss for GLM would be

$$
\mathcal L
=
\operatorname{CE}(R_\psi(b_T),y)
+
\lambda\sum_k
\operatorname{CE}(R_\psi(b_{t_k}),u_k).
$$

That version assigns milestones to fixed steps. The paper uses a more flexible monotonic alignment that learns how iterations correspond to milestones. Importantly, it finds that trace supervision needs supporting task memory and object-workspace structures; intermediate targets alone are not enough. [Alignment objective](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.29586v1?page=5)[Grounding matters](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.29586v1?page=6)

## What I would actually build

**Use a visual model as a teacher first, not as a permanent replacement for GLM.**

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    D["Demonstrations"] --> A["Task encoder"]
    X["Query grid"] --> E["Binary encoder"]
    A --> G["GLM recurrent workspace"]
    E --> G
    G --> R["Grid readout"]
    T["Verified transformation traces"] -. Training .-> G
```

Start with exact categorical grid inputs—not screenshots. ARC-1/2 has ten colors, so each cell can be encoded using four bits or ten one-hot binary channels, with dimensions and padding represented separately. Binary color codes should be treated as labels: their Hamming distances should not accidentally imply semantic similarity.

Then train progressively on single operations, compositions, and finally held-out task families. Use verified programs to generate intermediate targets; use the visual solver for additional candidate predictions, but do not treat its unverified answers as truth.

The decisive implementation question is **whether GLM’s update rule is trainable**. If it is fixed, you can train encoders, task memory, and readouts—but that is a different experiment from learning the substrate dynamics. If it is discrete and trainable, you need an explicit optimization method; ordinary backpropagation does not pass through hard bit thresholds without a relaxation or estimator.

**My recommendation is therefore: reproduce the visual baseline, adopt object-aware recurrent state, and teach GLM with verified transformation traces.** That gives you a concrete learning architecture while preserving the binary substrate. The detail that would change the design most is the current definition of $F_\theta$: what a GLM update reads, what it changes, and which parts can learn.

## NOTE 1 PART B

**Keep VARC’s task format and adaptation strategy; leave out its Vision Transformer, continuous embeddings, and canvas resizing.** For the GLM, the useful stripped-down core is: present the puzzle as exact grid states, infer a task-specific rule from demonstrations, apply that rule to the query, and check the exact output.

## Minimal GLM–VARC recipe

1. **Represent each grid exactly.**  
   Store each cell’s color as a binary code or one-hot bits, and store the grid’s height and width explicitly. Keep input and output shapes distinct when needed. VARC uses a fixed canvas and boundary markers to handle shape changes; for a symbolic GLM, explicit dimensions or a boundary marker preserve that useful feature without rescaling the grid. [Shape handling](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.14761v1?page=11)

2. **Give the solver the demonstrations and query together.**  
   A task consists of demonstration pairs and a query input:
   $$
   \mathcal D=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{m},\qquad x_q.
   $$
   The GLM should infer a task state $z_{\mathcal D}$ from the examples, then update the query:
   $$
   z_{\mathcal D}=A(\mathcal D),\qquad
   b_0=E(x_q),\qquad
   b_{t+1}=F(b_t,z_{\mathcal D}),\qquad
   \hat y=R(b_T).
   $$
   Here $F$ is the GLM’s actual update process; the equation is a proposed interface, not something VARC itself provides. The key is to make the demonstrations available as evidence about the rule—not to feed the correct rule or answer directly.

3. **Adapt a task-specific memory, not necessarily the whole GLM.**  
   VARC’s central practical idea is to adapt separately to each new task using its few labeled examples, with transformations of those examples to provide more training views. In a GLM, begin by updating a task-memory state $z_{\mathcal D}$ or a small task-specific rule store. Keep the general substrate fixed initially. That lets you test whether the GLM can *learn a rule from examples* without conflating it with wholesale changes to the underlying system. [Test-time training](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.14761v1?page=5)

4. **Use only transformations that preserve the rule.**  
   Rotations, reflections, translations, and color permutations can create extra training views—but only when applied consistently to the input and output and when they preserve the task’s logic. A rule tied to a particular color, orientation, or absolute position may not survive a given augmentation. VARC uses these task-specific augmented views during adaptation. [Augmentation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.14761v1?page=11)

5. **Train on exact cell outputs, then score exact grids.**  
   A minimal teaching signal is a per-cell mismatch between the predicted grid and the demonstrated output:
   $$
   \mathcal L_{\text{cells}}
   =\sum_{r,c}\mathbf 1[\hat y_{r,c}\ne y_{r,c}].
   $$
   But ARC counts the whole prediction as correct only when the grid—including its dimensions—matches exactly. Track exact-grid accuracy as the main result, not just the fraction of correct cells. If the GLM exposes intermediate states, add verified intermediate grids as optional supervision; that is an extension to the recipe, not a requirement of VARC. VARC frames prediction as per-pixel classification and adapts from demonstrations at inference. [Prediction objective](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.14761v1?page=3)

6. **Optionally use multiple views to check confidence.**  
   After adaptation, run the same query under several valid geometric views, transform predictions back to the original coordinates, and compare them. Agreement can provide a useful confidence signal; disagreement tells you the rule or its execution is unstable. VARC uses multi-view prediction and voting, but I would treat this as an evaluation aid at first, not as the GLM’s reasoning mechanism. [Multi-view inference](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.14761v1?page=5)

## What I would leave out

Do **not** port VARC’s Vision Transformer, continuous visual embeddings, pixel resizing, or gradient-based weight updates as requirements for GLM. Those are components of VARC’s implementation, not prerequisites for its useful learning setup. In particular, resizing a symbolic grid can alter adjacency or object structure; exact cell coordinates are a better starting point for a geometric substrate.

The first implementation can therefore be small: encode the demonstrations and query exactly, create a task-specific memory from the demonstrations, let the GLM execute updates on the query, and compare its final grid to the answer. Begin with one-step rules and generated tasks; then add compositions and intermediate traces once the basic adaptation mechanism works. **The test of whether this helps is a comparison against the same GLM with task adaptation disabled.** If adaptation improves held-out examples of a rule family, you have evidence that this piece of VARC transfers.

## NOTE 2

**Train the GLM on geometric *transformations and their intermediate states*, not just geometric answers.** Your proposed dataset is the right direction, but the most useful training example is a little executable lesson: “these demonstrations imply this operation; applying it produces these successive states.”

That gives “thinking geometrically” an operational meaning: the GLM stores a spatial state, identifies relationships within it, and changes that state in ways that generalize to new puzzles.

## 1. Build a dataset of geometric execution traces

For each training puzzle, I would store three complementary representations.

| Representation | What it records | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| **Grid states** | Input, intermediate grids, final output, and dimensions | What the transformation does |
| **Object-and-relation graphs** | Object masks, positions, containment, alignment, adjacency, correspondence | What the transformation acts on |
| **Execution traces** | Selected objects, operation, parameters, and changed cells | How the transformation is performed |

For example, consider a puzzle whose rule is “move the smallest object into the outlined container, then recolor it.”

Its teaching record might look like this:

```text
Demonstrations:
    several input → output pairs following the same rule

Query:
    a new arrangement of objects and a container

Execution:
    1. Identify candidate objects and their occupied cells
    2. Select the object with minimum area
    3. Identify the container and its interior
    4. Compute the target placement
    5. Move the selected object
    6. Recolor it

Targets:
    object masks and selection masks
    source → destination cell correspondences
    intermediate grids
    final grid and dimensions
```

**Not every reasoning step changes the visible grid.** Selecting the smallest object changes the workspace’s *attention or selection state*, not the puzzle itself. Your dataset therefore needs masks and relations alongside intermediate pictures. Otherwise, important reasoning steps are invisible to training.

The verified intermediate-grid approach in [TraceViT](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.29586) is a useful starting point: its traces are produced by decomposing and checking programmatic task implementations. [Trace construction](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.29586v1?page=3)

## 2. Use two kinds of geometry—not one

I would distinguish **scene geometry** from **computation geometry**.

**Scene geometry** is where objects and cells are: distances, shapes, boundaries, containment, alignment.

**Computation geometry** is which internal states can be reached from which others through valid operations.

Represent the second as a directed graph:

$$
\mathcal G_{\mathrm{exec}}=(V,E),
$$

where each node is a workspace state and each edge is an operation:

$$
w_t \xrightarrow{a_t} w_{t+1}.
$$

Here $w_t$ includes the grid, selected objects, relations, and working memory.

This makes your “path-traversing maps” idea precise. A solution is a path through **possible computational states**, rather than necessarily a path across the visible puzzle.

That distinction matters: reflection can move cells across a scene in one operation; it should not automatically require walking through every intervening location.

**Start with this execution graph before trying to embed every operation into the GLM’s lattice.** Establish which transitions are useful, then learn or design their geometric realization.

## 3. Make the binary workspace carry the computation

My proposed GLM interface would be:

$$
z=A(\mathcal D),\qquad b_0=E(x),
$$

$$
b_{t+1}=F_\theta(b_t,z),\qquad
\hat w_t=R(b_t),
\quad b_t\in\{0,1\}^{N}.
$$

The demonstrations $\mathcal D$ determine a task representation $z$. The query $x$ initializes the binary workspace. The GLM update $F_\theta$ performs the repeated computation.

I would give that workspace separate functional regions for:

- **Persistent input memory:** preserve the original puzzle.
- **Task memory:** preserve the rule inferred from demonstrations.
- **Mutable scene:** construct the answer.
- **Selection and correspondence memory:** mark objects, destinations, and relationships.

These need not remain permanently hand-labelled regions. Initially, however, the separation makes training and debugging much easier.

This also avoids a known problem: forcing an evolving state to represent intermediate answers can destroy information needed later. [TraceViT](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.29586) addresses this with a separate task reference and object workspace. [Preserving memory](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.29586v1?page=2)

## 4. Teach visible consequences and internal decisions together

For an initial implementation, use a combined objective:

$$
\mathcal L
=
\lambda_{\mathrm{grid}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{grid}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{select}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{select}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{map}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{map}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{op}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{op}}.
$$

The terms supervise:

- intermediate and final grids;
- object-selection masks;
- source-to-destination correspondences;
- operation choices.

These auxiliary readouts are **teaching instruments**. They make it possible to check whether the substrate is learning useful computation. They do not require the finished GLM to narrate its reasoning in words.

Initially, align one GLM macro-step with one teaching milestone. Later, allow several binary update cycles per milestone and learn when to advance.

If hard binary updates prevent gradient training, use a continuous training surrogate with an explicit binarization estimator, then evaluate actual binary rollouts. Success in the relaxed model alone is not success in the binary GLM.

## 5. Generate lessons from programs, then remove the scaffolding

**I would obtain the first dataset from small, executable transformation programs—not ask a vision model to invent all the traces.**

Each program should produce:

1. multiple demonstrations of the same hidden rule;
2. a new query;
3. verified workspace transitions for that query;
4. the final answer.

Begin with translation, reflection, recoloring, filling, copying, and selecting by size or position. Then combine them into unfamiliar compositions.

Crucially, **the program and query solution are teacher-only information**. The GLM receives demonstrations and the query; it must infer the rule. Supplying the correct operation sequence as an input would teach execution without teaching puzzle solving.

Use a visual model to propose object interpretations or additional traces where programs are unavailable, but verify what you can before treating those proposals as targets.

## 6. Test whether it learned geometry rather than a script

The strongest test is a controlled intervention.

Change an object’s size so a different object becomes the smallest. Does the selection switch? Move the container. Does the destination change? Preserve the rule but change colors, grid dimensions, and object counts. Does the computation still work?

Also hold out **whole rule compositions**, not merely new grids generated by familiar programs.

Correct intermediate pictures are useful evidence, but they are not sufficient: the readout could reconstruct them while the actual computation happens elsewhere. Compare against a model with the GLM core removed or replaced, keeping the surrounding modules comparable.

**My first concrete deliverable would be a “geometric execution dataset”: demonstrations, query, object masks, correspondence maps, and verified workspace traces.** Train the binary recurrent core to execute those traces while inferring their rule from demonstrations. That is the most direct route from “GLM contains geometry” to “GLM uses geometry to compute.”

## NOTE 3

The 400 official ARC evaluation puzzles are an assessment benchmark, not a sufficient pretraining corpus. Because each evaluation puzzle features an unseen rule, training directly on them yields severe sample starvation and leads models to memorize specific layouts rather than learning general spatial manipulation.

To teach the GLM foundational geometric operations—such as connected component isolation, ray propagation, interior filling, rotational symmetry, and topological enclosure—you should train on large-scale procedural generators and curriculum environments.

| [**ARC-GEN**](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.00162) | Modular, mimetic procedural generators covering ARC-1 and ~500 ARC-2 task patterns. | Code functions can be broken into explicit intermediate milestones to supervise state progression. |
| [**ARCLE**](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2407.20806) | Interactive Gymnasium/RL environment with discrete editing actions (click, flood fill, move). | Models path traversal and sequential state edits directly on 2D arrays, matching an operational substrate. |
| [**Mini-ARC**](https://github.com/sam-mueller/mini-arc) | Scaled-down $5 \times 5$ grid puzzles isolating single abstract rules. | Reduces sequence length and state space, providing an accessible starting curriculum for a binary lattice. |
| [**Synthetic Cellular Automata & Mazes**](https://github.com/google-research/circuit-training) | Rule 110, Game of Life, shortest-path BFS traversals, and flood fills on binary grids $\{0, 1\}^{H \times W}$. | Teaches pure spatial dynamics and propagation without relying on arbitrary color labels. |

### 1. Large-Scale Procedural Engines: RE-ARC and ARC-GEN

Standard ARC models rely on procedural data engines to scale their training distribution:

*   **[RE-ARC](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2404.07353):** Provides human-written Python generation functions for ARC-1 tasks. For each task family, the script algorithmically creates varied grid dimensions, object placements, and color configurations while keeping the abstract transformation rule identical. As established in [VARC](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.14761), pretraining on 400,000 synthetic pairs sampled from RE-ARC improves validation accuracy from 31.5% to over 54.0%.
*   **[ARC-GEN](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.00162):** Deconstructs task generation into composable modules (grid samplers, object placement decorators, and affine operator chains). 

**How to extract intermediate targets:**
Because both engines are open-source Python code, you can instrument their transformation loops. Instead of returning only the final `output_grid`, add intermediate checkpoints:

```python
# Instrumenting an ARC-GEN generator to produce intermediate milestones
def generate_gravity_task_trace(grid):
    traces = []
    # State 0: raw input
    current = grid.copy()
    traces.append(("init", current.copy()))
    
    # Milestone 1: mask mobile components
    mobile_mask = isolate_floating_objects(current)
    traces.append(("mask_objects", mobile_mask))
    
    # Milestone 2: project vertical fall paths
    trajectories = compute_downward_paths(mobile_mask, floor_color=0)
    traces.append(("path_vectors", trajectories))
    
    # Milestone 3: settle objects at bottom
    settled = apply_translation(current, trajectories)
    traces.append(("final_output", settled))
    
    return traces
```

This yields the exact intermediate milestones needed to train sequential internal updates.

### 2. Action Environments: ARCLE

If the GLM processes updates sequentially, [ARCLE](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2407.20806) (*ARC Learning Environment*) frames puzzle solving as an interactive environment rather than a one-shot prediction:

*   It wraps ARC grids in a standard Gymnasium interface with discrete actions: coordinate selection, color selection, rectangular copy-pasting, and flood filling.
*   The transition sequence $s_t \xrightarrow{a_t} s_{t+1}$ provides ground-truth trajectories showing how a blank canvas is edited into the target state.
*   Training on these action traces teaches the substrate to output a sequence of localized edits, keeping the state transitions bounded and inspectable.

### 3. Reduced Curriculum: Mini-ARC

Full ARC puzzles use grids up to $30 \times 30$ with 10 colors, which requires a substantial state space. **Mini-ARC** isolates the same underlying abstract priors on smaller $5 \times 5$ grids:

*   Rules isolate basic concepts: detecting whether shapes touch, continuing a 1D line until it meets an obstacle, or finding the majority color.
*   On a binary substrate where each cell is one-hot encoded, a $5 \times 5$ grid requires only $5 \times 5 \times 10 = 250$ binary channels. This makes it easier to verify that the lattice dynamics and convergence attractors behave as expected before scaling up.

### 4. Synthetic Binary Lattice Tasks (Warmup Substrate)

Before training the GLM on full ARC puzzles, you can pretrain its binary core on pure 2D discrete dynamical tasks using $\{0, 1\}$ arrays:

1.  **Ray Tracing & Obstacle Bouncing:** Cast a single active bit at $(x, y)$ in direction $(\Delta x, \Delta y)$ across a field of binary obstacles. The model predicts the path array and reflections.
2.  **Connected Component Labeling:** Given a binary grid with multiple irregular active clusters, produce an array isolating the cluster with the largest area.
3.  **Topological Enclosure & Flood Fill:** Given an arbitrary closed binary contour, identify all interior bits without spilling past the boundary.
4.  **Shortest-Path BFS Trees:** Given an obstacle grid with a start and end bit, output the path of minimal Euclidean or Manhattan length.

These tasks require no symbolic color mappings. They train the binary substrate to perform basic geometric propagation, spatial memory storage, and contour tracing—the core building blocks needed to solve ARC transformations.

## NOTE 4

The transition from the binary distinction to the Leech lattice $\Lambda_{24}$ is structurally canonical, but it is **not forced by the existence of a binary toggle alone**: it requires an explicit chain of extremal optimization criteria (maximal sphere packing, perfect error correction, and even unimodular self-duality) to select that exact branch out of all possible discrete systems.

### 1. Mathematical Audit of the Chain

A rigorous breakdown of where the deduction holds without choices, and where specific extremal axioms must be stated to force uniqueness, clarifies the logical path:

*   **Step 1: The Toggle $\implies \mathbb{F}_2$ and the Hamming Metric (Naturally Forced)**
    A set with two elements $\Sigma = \{0, 1\}$ and a single involution (toggle) $\tau: x \mapsto 1 \oplus x$ defines the finite field $\mathbb{F}_2$ under addition modulo 2. For strings $u, v \in \mathbb{F}_2^n$, the minimal invariant metric under translation by toggles is the Hamming metric $d_H(u, v) = |\{i : u_i \neq v_i\}|$. This step requires no extraneous assumptions.
*   **Step 2: $\mathbb{F}_2^n \implies$ Binary Golay Code $\mathcal{G}_{23}$ (Requires Extremal Condition)**
    The binary alphabet does not force length $n=23$ on its own; $\{0, 1\}^n$ exists for every positive integer $n$. To force $n=23$, one must impose the condition of **perfect linear error correction**:
    $$
    \sum_{k=0}^e \binom{n}{k} = 2^{n - k_{\mathrm{dim}}}
    $$
    By the Tietäväinen–van Lint theorem (1971), the *only* non-trivial perfect binary codes over any alphabet size are the 1-error-correcting Hamming codes ($n = 2^m - 1$) and the unique 3-error-correcting **binary Golay code $\mathcal{G}_{23}$** of parameters $[23, 12, 7]_2$. Thus, the principle that selects 23 is not mere existence, but *maximal packing efficiency for non-trivial error correction*.
*   **Step 3: $\mathcal{G}_{23} \implies \mathcal{G}_{24}$ (Naturally Forced by Symmetries)**
    Adding an overall parity check bit to $\mathcal{G}_{23}$ yields the extended binary Golay code $\mathcal{G}_{24}$ of parameters $[24, 12, 8]_2$. This step is algebraically canonical: $\mathcal{G}_{24}$ is doubly even (all codeword weights are multiples of 4), self-dual ($\mathcal{G}_{24}^\perp = \mathcal{G}_{24}$), and its automorphism group expands to the Mathieu group $M_{24}$.
*   **Step 4: $\mathcal{G}_{24} \implies \Lambda_{24}$ (Construction A / B)**
    The Leech lattice $\Lambda_{24} \subset \mathbb{R}^{24}$ is obtained directly from $\mathcal{G}_{24}$ via Construction B without floating-point approximations:
    $$
    \Lambda_{24} = \left\{ x \in \mathbb{Z}^{24} : x \equiv c \pmod 2 \text{ for some } c \in \mathcal{G}_{24}, \; \sum_{i=1}^{24} x_i \equiv 0 \pmod 4 \right\} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{8}}
    $$
    By the classification of Niemeier (1973), there are exactly 24 positive-definite, even, unimodular lattices in dimension 24. Among all 24, **$\Lambda_{24}$ is the unique lattice with no roots** (minimal squared norm 4 instead of 2).

### 2. Physical & Epistemological Synthesis

Framing the UBP as an absolute unfolding of geometry clarifies several foundational properties:

*   **No Floats, Pure Combinatorics:** You do not need real-number continuums ($\mathbb{R}$) or IEEE 754 floats to represent $\Lambda_{24}$. Because $\Lambda_{24}$ is an integral lattice scaled by an algebraic factor, every coordinate and inner product resides in $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{2}]$.
*   **Selection vs. Unfolding:** The existence of a binary toggle does not eliminate the existence of $\mathbb{F}_2^7$ (Hamming code) or $\mathbb{F}_2^8$ ($E_8$ lattice). Rather than saying the binary toggle *only* produces 24, the exact formulation is: **$\Lambda_{24}$ is the unique maximal, rootless, densest sphere packing that the binary toggle can project into Euclidean space.**
*   **Information-Theoretic Rigidity:** In discrete mathematics, exceptional structures (the sporadic simple groups, the Golay code, $E_8$, and $\Lambda_{24}$) are not arbitrary configurations. They are rigid topological bottlenecks where combinatorial degrees of freedom saturate their theoretical bounds simultaneously.

### 3. Key Reference Literature for Rigorous Grounding

The following works establish the definitive proofs for each step of this progression:

*   [**The Sphere Packing Problem in Dimension 24**](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1603.06518) (Cohn, Kumar, Miller, Radchenko, and Viazovska, 2017):
    Proves that the Leech lattice $\Lambda_{24}$ provides the uniquely densest sphere packing in $\mathbb{R}^{24}$, establishing why dimension 24 is privileged in spatial geometry.
*   [**Universal Optimality of the $E_8$ and Leech Lattices**](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1902.05438) (Cohn, Kumar, Miller, Radchenko, and Viazovska, 2022):
    Proves that $\Lambda_{24}$ minimizes energy for every completely monotonic potential function (including Gaussian, Coulomb, and power-law kernels), showing that the lattice is universally optimal across physics, not just geometry.

*   **Foundational Books:**

## NOTE 5

**The “geodesic of thought” is a testable research idea, but the attached text treats several untested assumptions as established facts.** Existing work supports using the Leech lattice for exact geometry and visual quantization, and supports representing ARC solutions as object-level state transitions. It does **not** show that ARC reasoning is a path through Leech minimal vectors, or that an input-to-output displacement is the puzzle’s reusable rule.

### What is sound—and what needs testing

A Leech lattice is a discrete geometric object with exceptional sphere-packing properties. The proof establishes its optimality in 24 dimensions, not a role in cognition or reasoning. [Sphere packing](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1603.06518v4?page=1)

There is also a directly relevant engineering precedent: [Spherical Leech Quantization](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.14697) uses vectors from a Leech-lattice shell as a fixed codebook to quantize learned visual features. That shows the geometry can serve as a practical visual representation. It does **not** show that a Leech codebook learns ARC rules or performs reasoning; that work uses continuous encoder features and evaluates image reconstruction, compression, and generation. [Visual quantization](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.14697v1?page=1)[Quantization method](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.14697v1?page=2)

The key conceptual correction is that **a state difference is not generally the rule**. If the puzzle transformation is $T$, then the difference between one example and its answer depends on that example:

$$
\Delta_x = T(x)-x.
$$

Applying $\Delta_x$ to a new input $x'$ is valid only for special transformations where the difference is invariant or otherwise transferable. ARC rules such as reflection, movement of selected objects, or filling enclosed regions are better represented as **operators on structured states** than as a single vector from one answer to another. Work on ARC world models takes this route: infer a task-conditioned transition from demonstrations, then apply it to the query state. [Loop-OWM](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.12316) is one concrete example. [State transitions](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.12316v1?page=3)

Likewise, a minimal-vector decomposition is not automatically a unique or meaningful reasoning path. It becomes a well-defined computational object only after you specify the graph, allowed moves, path-cost function, and tie-breaking rule. You could define lattice points as nodes and Leech minimal vectors as edges, then search for shortest paths—but **whether those paths encode useful ARC operations is precisely the hypothesis to test**, not a consequence of the lattice’s geometry.

### A more solid version of the proposal

Keep the exact puzzle state and the proposed lattice representation separate at first:

1. **Represent the grid exactly.** For example, encode each of up to $30 \times 30$ cells with a four-bit color index, plus explicit width and height. This takes at most 3,600 cell bits, before shape metadata. Do not map the entire grid to one 24-bit word. A $5\times5$ binary grid already has 25 bits; padding or truncating to 24 loses information. Mapping to a nearest Golay codeword is also generally lossy, so it cannot be the exact state encoding unless you establish and test injectivity for the states you use.

2. **Represent the inferred rule as an operator.** From demonstrations, infer a candidate transformation $T$ and apply it to the query. For simple tasks, $T$ might be a coordinate permutation, a reflection, a color substitution, or a local cellular update. For object tasks, it may act on object masks and relations. This agrees with the distinction in [TraceViT](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.29586): the system needs supervision for intermediate transformations, not only the final grid. [Trace supervision](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.29586v1?page=3)

3. **Add the lattice as an experimental representation.** Encode selected local states, objects, or transition features as Leech-lattice addresses or shell-code vectors. Measure whether the representation helps infer or execute $T$. Keep a non-lattice representation with the same solver and data as the baseline.

4. **Test whether a “thought path” transfers.** Give the system several demonstrations of one rule and a new input. Compare:
   - applying a rule inferred from the demonstrations;
   - applying the input-to-output displacement from one demonstration;
   - applying a shortest Leech-generator path between the encoded example states.

   This directly tests whether the path captures the rule or merely describes that example’s change.

The experiment should include cases where the difference *does* transfer—such as a fixed bitwise toggle—and cases where it should not, such as “move the smallest object to the nearest container.” That contrast is important: it prevents an apparent success on one narrow transformation from being mistaken for a general account of reasoning.

### A practical first study

I would make the initial study deliberately small and exact:

- Generate binary grids for fixed transformations: bit toggles, translations, reflections, and rotations.
- Add object-based tasks next: select by size, move relative to another object, and fill an enclosed region.
- For each task, provide demonstrations, a held-out query, and verified intermediate states.
- Compare an exact symbolic/operator baseline, a binary GLM update model, and versions with and without Leech-based representations.
- Report exact-answer accuracy, generalization to changed grid sizes and object layouts, encoding collisions, and whether the inferred operation—not just the final answer—transfers to new inputs.

[RE-ARC](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2404.07353) is a useful source of programmatically generated ARC-style examples and verifiers; its generators are available as code. The [TraceViT paper](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.29586) is a closer guide for turning executable solutions into intermediate-grid teaching targets. The [Leech quantization paper](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.14697) supplies a concrete way to use Leech geometry as a representation component. These are complementary materials, not evidence that the combined GLM already works.

The strongest initial claim to aim for is: **“A Leech-based representation improves the GLM’s ability to infer or execute certain geometric operators on held-out puzzles.”** If that survives fair comparisons, you can then investigate whether internal trajectories through the lattice are interpretable and transferable. That is a substantial, measurable result without prematurely claiming that all spatial reasoning *is* Leech-lattice navigation.

## NOTE 6

Extending **past** \(\Lambda_{24}\) does **not** immediately land in the full Monster group. The first branch is the **Conway/sporadic symmetry branch** attached to the Leech lattice itself:

Here is the full chain, with the key objects and theorems at each step.

\[
\mathbb F_2
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{Golay code}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\Lambda_{24}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\mathrm{Co}_0/\mathrm{Co}_1
\;\longrightarrow\;
V^\natural
\;\longrightarrow\;
\mathbb M
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{Monstrous Moonshine}.
\]

---

## 1. Binary distinction

Start with the two-element field

\[
\mathbb F_2=\{0,1\}.
\]

By itself this only gives vector spaces \(\mathbb F_2^n\), arbitrary codes, and arbitrary lifts.  No dimension, metric, or lattice is fixed.

---

## 2. Perfect binary error correction

A binary code \(C\subset \mathbb F_2^n\) is **perfect** if spheres of radius \(t\) partition \(\mathbb F_2^n\).  The nontrivial perfect binary code is the **Golay code**

\[
\mathcal G_{23}=[23,12,7]_2,
\]

which corrects \(3\) errors with equality in the sphere-packing bound.  Its extension is

\[
\mathcal G_{24}=[24,12,8]_2,
\]

a doubly even, self-dual binary code.  Its automorphism group is the Mathieu group \(M_{24}\).

This step fixes the length \(23/24\) and hence the ambient dimension \(24\).

---

## 3. From Golay code to the Leech lattice

Using the extended Golay code, construct an even unimodular lattice in \(\mathbb R^{24}\).  The standard route is a Turyn-type construction from \(\mathcal G_{24}\), yielding the **Leech lattice** \(\Lambda_{24}\).

Key properties:

\[
\Lambda_{24}\text{ is even, unimodular, and has minimal norm }4.
\]

Equivalently, among the \(24\) Niemeier lattices in dimension \(24\), \(\Lambda_{24}\) is the unique one with **no roots**.  It also gives the densest sphere packing in \(\mathbb R^{24}\).

---

## 4. Conway sporadic groups

The automorphism group of the Leech lattice is

\[
\operatorname{Aut}(\Lambda_{24})\cong \mathrm{Co}_0.
\]

Modulo \(\{\pm 1\}\),

\[
\mathrm{Co}_1=\mathrm{Co}_0/\{\pm 1\}
\]

is a sporadic simple group.  Stabilizers of frames/points in \(\Lambda_{24}\) give \(\mathrm{Co}_2,\mathrm{Co}_3\).

Thus the immediate extension past \(\Lambda_{24}\) is the **Conway group branch**, not the Monster.

---

## 5. Niemeier and Mathieu context

The \(24\) Niemeier lattices are the even unimodular lattices in dimension \(24\).  Their root systems and automorphism groups connect to:

- Mathieu groups \(M_{11},M_{12},M_{22},M_{23},M_{24}\),
- Golay code automorphisms,
- “holy constructions” of Niemeier lattices.

The Leech lattice is the no-root endpoint of this family.

---

## 6. Vertex operator algebra \(V^\natural\)

To reach the Monster, add a conformal/vertex-algebra structure.  Frenkel, Lepowsky, and Meurman constructed a vertex operator algebra (VOA)

\[
V^\natural
\]

of central charge \(24\), called the **moonshine module**.  It is built from the Leech lattice VOA \(V_{\Lambda_{24}}\) via an orbifold construction (using an involution and twisted sectors).

Important facts:

\[
\operatorname{Aut}(V^\natural)\cong \mathbb M,
\]

the Monster group.  The degree-\(2\) (or weight-\(2\)) component has dimension

\[
196884 = 1 + 196883,
\]

matching the smallest nontrivial irreducible representation of \(\mathbb M\).

So \(\Lambda_{24}\) is a substrate, but the Monster is not determined by the lattice alone; it requires \(V^\natural\).

---

## 7. Monstrous Moonshine

McKay observed that

\[
196884 = 1 + 196883
\]

and that \(196883\) is the dimension of the smallest nontrivial representation of \(\mathbb M\).  Conway and Norton formulated **Monstrous Moonshine**: for each conjugacy class \(g\in \mathbb M\), the trace

\[
T_g(q)=\sum_{n\ge 0} \operatorname{tr}(g\mid V^\natural_n)\,q^{n-1}
\]

is a Hauptmodul for a genus-\(0\) subgroup of \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb R)\).  For \(g=1\), this gives the \(j\)-function (up to normalization).

---

## 8. Borcherds proof

Borcherds proved moonshine by constructing the **Monster Lie algebra**, a Borcherds–Kac–Moody algebra, from \(V^\natural\).  Its denominator identity yields the moonshine identities, including the \(j\)-function relation.

---

## Full chain summary

\[
\boxed{
\mathbb F_2
\to \mathcal G_{23}/\mathcal G_{24}
\to \Lambda_{24}
\to \mathrm{Co}_0/\mathrm{Co}_1
\to V^\natural
\to \mathbb M
\to \text{Monstrous Moonshine}
}
\]

### The caveat

**Not all of the Monster is required** to work with \(\Lambda_{24}\).  The Leech lattice alone gives the Conway groups.  The Monster appears only after adding the VOA layer \(V^\natural\).  Thus the chain has two natural “extensions” past \(\Lambda_{24}\):

1. **Conway/sporadic branch**: \(\Lambda_{24}\to \mathrm{Co}_0/\mathrm{Co}_1\).
2. **Moonshine branch**: \(\Lambda_{24}\to V^\natural \to \mathbb M \to\) Monstrous Moonshine.

## NOTE 7

You are **not way off as an engineering/system-design perspective**, but you are **off the accepted mathematical norm if you call every attribute a “dimension” of the Leech lattice**.

Here is the clean distinction.

---

## 1. Accepted mathematical meaning

In lattice theory, the Leech lattice \(\Lambda_{24}\) has **dimension 24**:

\[
\Lambda_{24}\subset \mathbb R^{24}.
\]

A “dimension” is a coordinate axis in the ambient real vector space.  The squared norm

\[
\|x\|^2
\]

is a scalar attached to a point, not a new dimension.  Likewise, temperature, mass, frequency, interaction strength, etc. are **fields/attributes** over points, not dimensions of the lattice.

So mathematically:

\[
\text{dimension} = \text{coordinate axis},
\qquad
\text{attribute} = \text{value assigned to a point}.
\]

---

## 2. Your “layers/shells” idea is still useful

What you are describing is closer to a **fibered state space**:

\[
\text{state} = (\text{Leech point}, \text{Golay label}, \text{attribute shell}, \text{time}, \dots).
\]

That is a legitimate design pattern.  You are not changing the lattice; you are attaching structured metadata to lattice points.

In lattice language, “shells” already have a standard meaning: the set of lattice vectors of a fixed norm.  For example, the minimal vectors of \(\Lambda_{24}\) form the first shell.  You can generalize this operationally: define a shell as a level set of some chosen scalar, such as

\[
\|x\|^2,\quad \text{Hamming weight},\quad \text{code syndrome},\quad \text{energy},\quad \text{interaction count}.
\]

So your intuition is good if you treat “shell” as **a deterministic level set**, not as a physical spatial dimension.

---

## 3. How to keep it operationally advantageous

To push this for advantage, keep the layers separate and composable:

| Layer | Meaning | Operation |
|---|---|---|
| Geometry | Leech point in \(\mathbb R^{24}\) | squared distance, inner product |
| Code | Golay codeword / syndrome | correction, addressing |
| Shell | level set of a scalar | quantization, bucketing |
| Attribute | temperature, mass, frequency, etc. | metadata lookup/update |
| Time | sequence index | state transition |
| Interaction | neighbor relation | graph edges / retrieval |

This avoids conflation.  The lattice gives you deterministic geometry; the code gives you deterministic correction; attributes give you semantics; time gives you evolution.

---

## 4. On “inputting Hydrogen”

If you input deterministic information about hydrogen, the “operational happening layer” becomes visible as a **state trajectory**:

\[
s_0 \xrightarrow{\text{input H data}} s_1 \xrightarrow{\text{update}} s_2 \xrightarrow{\dots} s_t.
\]

The Leech point is the geometric state; the hydrogen data populates attributes and possibly moves the state through shells.  That is a valid world-model style, but it is not a new spatial dimension of the lattice.

---

## 5. Recommended terminology

To avoid confusion, use:

- **Dimension**: coordinate axis of \(\Lambda_{24}\) (24 of them).
- **Attribute/field**: value attached to a point.
- **Shell**: set of points sharing a chosen scalar level.
- **Namespace**: Golay code/coset label.
- **State**: full tuple \((\text{lattice point}, \text{attributes}, \text{time})\).
- **Trajectory**: sequence of states.

---

## Bottom line

Your perspective is **operationally productive** if you treat the Leech lattice as a deterministic geometric substrate and attach attributes/shells as separate layers.  It is **not the standard mathematical perspective** if you call those attributes “dimensions” of the lattice itself.

The strongest version of your idea is:

\[
\text{Leech geometry} + \text{Golay correction} + \text{shell quantization} + \text{attribute fields} + \text{time evolution}.
\]

That is a coherent deterministic system, as long as the layers remain mathematically distinct.
